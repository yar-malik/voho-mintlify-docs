Every production line needs a path to a human. This is not a fallback for a weak agent — it is a requirement of a line people trust. The question is not whether to have one, but when it fires and what the caller experiences.

## When to hand off

Four triggers cover most cases.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="The caller asks" icon="hand">
    A caller who asks for a person should get one. Arguing produces complaints, and an agent that refuses twice has already lost the call.

    Handle a single deflection at most: "I can log that for you right now, or put you through — which would you prefer?" If they ask again, transfer.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Safety or urgency" icon="triangle-exclamation">
    Some reports must reach a person immediately regardless of what the agent could do. Name them explicitly in the instructions — smoke, injury, a trapped person, anything involving danger.

    These should transfer without any attempt to collect details first.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Out of scope" icon="arrow-right-from-bracket">
    A caller on the maintenance line asking about their invoice is not a failure. Transfer to the right team rather than improvising an answer.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Repeated failure" icon="rotate-left">
    If the agent has asked the same question three times and still has not understood, further attempts will not help. Poor line quality and strong accents both produce this pattern.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## What the caller hears

Say that a transfer is happening and why. Silence followed by hold music reads as a dropped call.

```text
"I'll put you through to someone who can help with that — one moment."
```

<Warning>
  Never transfer into an unattended queue. A handoff to nobody is worse than no handoff, because the caller has now spent time with an agent *and* lost the call. Confirm the destination is staffed during the hours your line runs.
</Warning>

## Pass the context across

The single biggest frustration in a transferred call is repeating yourself. Whoever picks up should receive what has already been established — what the caller wants, what has been collected, and why the transfer happened.

Where a live screen pop is not possible, sending the summary to the destination team as a message still saves the caller from starting over.

## After hours

Decide explicitly what happens when no human is available:

- **Take a message** — the agent collects details and promises a callback. Only if someone actually calls back.
- **Say when to call** — honest and cheap. "Our team is available Sunday to Thursday, eight to five."
- **Escalate anyway** — for genuine emergencies, an out-of-hours number.

<Tip>
  Whichever you choose, have the agent state it plainly. Callers accept being told the office is closed. They do not accept being asked for details that go nowhere.
</Tip>

## Measure it

Track your handoff rate. A rate climbing over time usually means callers are bringing requests the agent was never configured for — which is a signal about what to build next, not a reason to make transfers harder.

A rate near zero is not automatically good either. It can mean callers who wanted a person could not get one.
